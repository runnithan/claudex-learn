# Don't trade your bishop for a knight without a good reason

## TL;DR

When your bishop is challenged, don't swap it for their knight by reflex. The trade can hand them time and leave the squares of that bishop's color weak.

## Why it matters

In the opening video, Black pins with ...Bg4 and, when challenged, simply trades it for the knight. The trade gains nothing, helps White bring the queen out and wastes time. It also weakens Black's light squares: White plays Qb3, hitting both f7 and b7, and Black is already lost. Defending f7 lets White take on b7 with the a8 rook hanging, and one way of defending allows Bb5, pinning and winning the queen. The video stresses that all this trouble came on the light squares because the light-squared bishop was gone. Wikipedia agrees that losing a bishop weakens your control of squares of that color.

## How to apply

When your bishop is attacked, look for a retreat or another useful move before trading. Give up bishop for knight only when you gain something concrete. Wikipedia adds that if a fianchettoed bishop sheltering your king is traded, the pawns around it become especially easy to break into on the squares the bishop used to cover.

## Check yourself

Your bishop on g4 pins a knight, and your opponent plays h3 to challenge it. Taking the knight gains you nothing concrete. What does this lesson say to do, and what might go wrong on the light squares if you trade?

## Caveat

The video says a bishop is generally a little stronger than a knight. The Wikipedia article rates them roughly equal overall: bishops are better in open positions, knights in closed ones, and a knight on an outpost can be worth more than a bishop. The advice against trading 'for no good reason' holds either way, but the bishop is not automatically the better piece.
