# Control the center with pieces, not only with pawns

## TL;DR

The classic advice is to put pawns in the center, but pieces can control it from a distance. What matters is which central squares you attack, not only which ones you occupy.

## Why it matters

In the opening video's example, Black could play the classical ...d5, but an idea from Nimzowitsch also works. ...Bb4 pins White's knight, so the knight no longer controls the central squares, and White can't play e4 without losing the pawn. Black stops White from taking the center without putting a pawn there, then adds a bishop aimed at the same squares. Wikipedia explains the logic: a piece on a central square doesn't necessarily control the center (a centralized knight attacks no central squares), while a bishop can control it from afar. Hypermodern openings do this, often with a fianchettoed bishop, while classical openings use pawns.

## How to apply

Count which central squares each side attacks, including pieces. To stop an opponent's central pawn advance, look for a pin, or a piece aimed at the square, so that the advance would lose a pawn. The pawn move ...d5 is still perfectly fine; this is an alternative.

## Check yourself

Your opponent wants to push a pawn to e4. You can't put a pawn in the center yourself, but you can pin the knight that defends e4. How does this lesson say the pin helps?
