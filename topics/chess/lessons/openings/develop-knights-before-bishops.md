# Develop your knights before your bishops

## TL;DR

Bring the knights out first. Bishops already have some scope from their home squares, and their best square often depends on what the opponent does. The knight's square is usually clear from the start.

## Why it matters

The opening video gives two reasons. First, a knight on its starting square is completely passive, while a bishop there is already fairly active. Second, the bishop is more flexible. You often don't know its best square yet, so you play the move you'll need anyway (the knight) and decide on the bishop later. In the video's example the bishop waited, then developed to a square where it also guarded a knight that had just been attacked.

## How to apply

In the opening, play the knight move you're sure of first. Then choose the bishop's square from the position. In the video's example, if Black plays ...d6 a black knight becomes more vulnerable and the bishop can go and attack it; if not, the bishop goes to a diagonal where it adds pressure. Wikipedia's model development follows the same order: center pawns first, then knights to protect them, then bishops (protected by the knights) to pin enemy pieces, then castling.

It's move 3 and your knights and bishops are all still at home. You're sure your knight belongs on f3 but unsure where your bishop is best. Which piece does this lesson say to move first, and why?
